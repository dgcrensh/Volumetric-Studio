# Volumetric CAD Studio — Product Spec

Nov 18, 2026 · @Dallas

## Overview

The studio is a desktop CAD application for mechanical parts in which every design is a tree of **volumes**: bounded regions that own their contents completely and connect to their neighbours only through authored **connection surfaces**. Contents can be modelled by hand with parametric features, imported, or generated by a **procedural node graph**. Procedural volumes are first-class, not a plug-in.

The bet is encapsulation. Outside a volume, nothing can refer to its contents, so a global modelling problem becomes a tree of local ones. Contents can be regenerated, swapped or baked without breaking anything downstream.

**Audience:** a personal tool for one user, its author, for designing mechanical parts for 3D printing and personal projects. It won't be published or sold, so licensing obligations, support, onboarding and multi-user workflows are out of scope.

**Platform:** native desktop (Windows, macOS, Linux) with a local GPU. Units default to millimetres.

## Core concepts

A design is a tree of volumes. Each volume has a boundary, connection surfaces it must respect, contents it fully controls, and optional published outputs.

&#91;embedded content: volume model · two sibling volumes and one connection surface\]

The parent owns the shared connection surface and passes it to both volumes as a fixed input. Neither volume can see the other.

| Term | Definition |
| --- | --- |
| Volume | A bounded region with full authority over its contents |
| Boundary | The closed solid a volume fills; authored by the parent or generated by a parent procedure |
| Connection surface | A typed, named patch where a volume must meet a neighbour or the outside; a fixed input |
| Contents | Whatever fills the volume; private to the volume |
| Published output | A read-only value a volume exposes, such as mass or envelope |

**Kinds of contents.** Every kind obeys the same contract, so one kind can replace another without affecting anything outside the volume.

| Kind | Contents come from | Regenerates when | Edited by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Static | Imported or baked geometry | Never | Replacing the file |
| Parametric | Its own feature tree | Its inputs or features change | Editing in context |
| Procedural | A node graph (fields and meshes) | Its inputs, parameters or seed change | The node editor |
| Composite | Child volumes, authored or generated | Any child or layout input changes | Its own layout |

## Design rules

Seven rules hold for every kind of volume. The system enforces them; users cannot switch them off.

1. **Encapsulation.** Nothing outside a volume may refer to its contents. Outside references can target only the volume's boundary, its connection surfaces and its published outputs.
2. **Full authority.** A volume owns every point inside its boundary and decides what to put there.
3. **Fixed connection inputs.** Connection surfaces are inputs to a volume. A volume cannot change, widen or negotiate them. Automatically easing a design to fit its constraints is a later convenience, not part of the core model.
4. **No overlap.** Sibling volume boundaries must not intersect. Overlap is a layout error, caught at the parent before anything generates. Volumes never detect or react to intruders.
5. **Containment.** Generated or modelled contents must stay inside the volume's boundary, within tolerance. This is checked for every kind of contents, including imported geometry.
6. **One-way data flow.** Dependencies form a directed graph with no loops. A volume's published outputs may feed only things that are not upstream of it. Loops are rejected when they are authored, not at rebuild time.
7. **Determinism.** The same inputs always produce identical contents. Randomness uses a seed stored with the volume, and results never depend on evaluation order or threading.

Generated volumes follow the same rules. A procedure that creates child volumes must produce layouts that pass the same overlap and containment checks as an authored layout.

## Geometry and precision

Authored geometry uses 64-bit floats and generated geometry uses 32-bit floats. Adjacency is decided from the model's structure, not by measuring distances.

| Data | Precision | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Boundaries, connection surfaces, transforms, sketches | FP64 | Few objects; they carry correctness |
| Generated meshes, field samples, GPU evaluation, display | FP32 | High volume; precision is adequate |
| Validation (overlap, containment) | FP64 | Compares output against authored boundaries |

**Working space and tolerance.** The model space is a fixed box of ±1,000 mm (proposed) with an absolute linear tolerance of 1e-6 mm (proposed). Both are product settings, not values derived from machine epsilon. The smallest allowed feature should be at least 1,000 times the tolerance.

**Structural adjacency.** When two volumes share a connection surface, they share the same authored surface object, so they are adjacent by definition and no tolerance is involved. Geometric tolerance is used only for:

- faces of sibling volumes with no shared connection surface: penetration up to the tolerance counts as touching. Touching without a declared connection raises a warning.
- containment: contents may reach the boundary but may not cross it by more than the tolerance.

**Seam conformity.** Each connection surface defines how its seam is divided into edges and vertices. Every volume touching it, whether mesh, field or B-rep, must use that division when tessellating. A single mesh is then built by concatenating volumes and welding the seams, with no boolean operations.

## Authoring layer

Parametric modelling defines intent: boundaries, connection surfaces and global variables. Volume contents are filled in beneath that layer.

**Sketches and features.** History-based parametric modelling in the SolidWorks style: constrained 2D sketches, features (extrude, revolve, sweep, loft, shell, pattern), reference geometry, and global variables and equations. It's used both at the layout level and inside parametric volumes.

**Defining volumes.** A volume's boundary is a closed solid produced by features or reference geometry. A boundary can be marked as a keep-out region for its siblings.

**Connection surfaces as named objects.** Connection surfaces are explicit, named objects built from sketch entities, datum planes or reference geometry, not picked from result faces. This keeps them stable when features above them change topology. Each connection surface has:

- **geometry:** a planar or curved patch on the boundary of each volume it joins;
- **type:** for example a bonded face, a bolted flange or a clearance face, with type-specific parameters (hole pattern, thread size, minimum wall);
- **seam definition:** how the seam is divided into edges and vertices (see Geometry and precision);
- **owner:** always the common parent of the volumes it joins, never one of those volumes.

**Edit in context.** Users open a parametric volume and edit its feature tree directly, much like editing a part within an assembly. Inside, features can refer to each other freely, and the usual topological naming fragility applies only within that volume. Internal sketches anchor to the volume's own boundary and connection surfaces, never to the parent's geometry.

**Cutting through a volume.** A cut that passes through a volume from outside is authored as a keep-out region that changes the volume's boundary before it generates. It is never a feature applied to the volume's contents afterwards.

## Procedural system

A procedural volume runs a node graph that takes the volume's boundary, its connection surfaces and its parameters, and produces its contents. Data in the graph is typed, and meshes and fields can both flow through it.

**Inputs.** The node graph receives the volume's boundary, its connection surfaces with their types and parameters, its own exposed parameters (which can be driven by global variables or configurations), and a seed. It receives nothing else: no neighbours and no parent geometry.

**Data types per edge.**

| Type | Used for | Converts to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Field (signed distance or scalar) | Infills, lattices, blends, offsets, grading | Mesh, by meshing against the seams |
| Mesh | Explicit geometry, instanced parts, final output | Field, by distance evaluation |
| Point set / frames | Scatter, patterns, placements | (used by instancing nodes) |
| Curves | Ribs, paths, routing inside the volume | Field or mesh by sweep |
| Scalars, vectors, lists | Parameters and published outputs | (none) |

Conversions are explicit nodes, so their cost and accuracy loss are visible in the graph.

**Initial node families.** Boundary and connection-surface access; field primitives and booleans; lattices and triply periodic minimal surfaces (TPMS); offset, shell and thicken; scatter and pattern; instance a template; meshing; queries (distance to boundary, distance to connection surface); math and logic.

**Generating child volumes.** A node can emit child volumes of any kind, for example many copies of a parametric bracket template, each sized by the procedure. Children receive boundaries and connection surfaces generated by the procedure, belong entirely to the parent, cannot be referred to from outside, and must pass the overlap and containment checks.

**Published outputs.** A procedure may publish read-only values (mass, volume, centre of gravity, bounding envelope, counts). These can feed only things that are not upstream of the volume, such as reports, checks or unrelated siblings.

**Baking.** A procedural volume can be baked into a static volume holding its current output. It can also be unbaked if the graph is kept.

## Evaluation engine

The whole model is one dependency graph. Each volume is a node that produces contents from its inputs, so only volumes whose inputs changed regenerate, and the authored layer never waits on generation.

**One scheduler for every kind of volume.** Static, parametric, procedural and composite volumes all appear to the scheduler as a node with inputs and contents. Static volumes never regenerate. Parametric volumes rebuild their feature tree. Procedural volumes run their graph. Composite volumes evaluate their children.

**Cache key.** A volume's contents are cached by a hash of its boundary, connection surfaces, parameters, seed, content definition (graph, feature tree or file) and engine version. An unchanged hash means no regeneration.

**Order of evaluation.**

1. Solve sketches and rebuild authored features, all on the main thread so the model always responds.
2. Validate the layout at each parent: checking boundaries for overlap is cheap and needs no generation.
3. Schedule volumes whose cache key changed, independent volumes in parallel on worker threads and the GPU.
4. Check each result for containment, then publish its outputs to anything downstream.
5. Weld seams and update the display.

**Interactive editing.** While a user drags a dimension, affected volumes show a preview: the boundary only, or a low-detail version. Full generation runs in the background when the drag ends, and a stale result is shown greyed out until its replacement arrives. Long generations can be cancelled.

**Determinism checks.** Debug builds can regenerate a volume twice and compare the hashes of the two outputs to catch nondeterminism.

## Validation and diagnostics

Every failure points to one place the user can act on: a layout, a connection surface, or a node in one volume.

| Check | When | Scope | Message names |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sibling overlap | Before generation | Parent | Both volumes, overlap region and depth |
| Undeclared contact | Before generation | Parent | Both faces; a warning, not an error |
| Dependency loop | When authored | Whole model | The full loop, as a path |
| Connection type mismatch | Before generation | Connection surface | The surface and the conflicting parameters |
| Containment | After generation | Volume | The volume and the region outside its boundary |
| Infeasible connections | During generation | Volume | The connection surfaces that can't all be met together |
| Node failure | During generation | Procedural graph | The node, its inputs and the error |
| Broken reference | Authored rebuild | Feature tree | The feature and its lost reference |

**Failed volumes.** A failed volume keeps its last valid contents, marked as stale, and blocks only what depends on it. The rest of the model keeps working.

**Seeing why.** In a procedural graph the user can open any node and see what it produced, drawn in place inside the volume. Infeasibility messages come from the procedure's own checks, so the node library needs a convention for reporting them: each node can report which of its inputs make it infeasible.

## Output and interop

Each volume exports in its natural representation, and a combined watertight mesh is always available for 3D printing.

| Export | Contents | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| STEP AP242 | Authored B-rep: layout, parametric volumes, boundaries | Procedural volumes go in as mesh bodies or as their boundary only (user's choice) |
| 3MF | Whole design as one welded mesh, or one mesh per volume | Main format for 3D printing; keeps volume names and metadata |
| STL | Whole design as one welded mesh | Legacy format for 3D printing |
| Native project | Everything, including graphs, seeds and caches | Caches are optional and can be rebuilt |

**Combined mesh.** Mesh each volume against its seam definitions, concatenate, and weld vertices that share a seam. Output is watertight and manifold by construction, with no boolean operations.

**Import.** STEP and IGES become static or parametric volumes (a feature tree where one can be recovered). STL, 3MF and OBJ become static mesh volumes. Imported geometry is checked for containment like any other contents.

**Mesh to B-rep (later).** Optional, approximate conversion of a procedural volume to B-rep for users who need STEP solids downstream. It is expensive and flagged as approximate.

**Drawings.** Drawings can show generated geometry. Dimensions placed on generated contents are disposable: they regenerate with the volume and can't drive anything. Dimensions on boundaries and connection surfaces are stable.

## User experience

The interface treats the volume as the unit of work. Users lay out volumes, connect them, then fill each one, in that order or iterating between them.

**Main views.**

- **Viewport:** 3D model; volumes shown with tinted boundaries, connection surfaces highlighted by type.
- **Volume tree:** the hierarchy of volumes, each marked with its kind (static, parametric, procedural, composite) and its status (current, stale, regenerating, failed).
- **Feature tree:** history for the layout, or for the volume being edited.
- **Node editor:** the graph for the procedural volume being edited, with a preview of any node's output.
- **Inspector:** parameters, connection-surface properties, published outputs, diagnostics.

**Core workflows.**

1. **Block out.** Sketch and extrude boundaries; place connection surfaces; leave the contents as placeholder volumes.
2. **Fill.** Choose a kind for each volume: model it in context, import it, or attach a procedure from a template library.
3. **Iterate.** Change a layout dimension and watch affected volumes preview, then regenerate.
4. **Swap.** Replace a volume's contents (placeholder → procedural → baked) without touching anything outside it.
5. **Ship.** Validate, then export STEP, 3MF or drawings.

**Habits users bring from other CAD tools.** Selecting a generated face and trying to fillet it, cut it or mate to it shows an inline explanation and offers the right action: edit the procedure's parameters, add a keep-out region, or mate to the connection surface instead.

## Scope and phasing

The MVP proves the volume contract with parametric layout and procedural infill for 3D-printed mechanical parts. Everything else builds on that.

**MVP**

- Parametric sketches and features, enough to author boundaries and connection surfaces
- Static, parametric and procedural volumes, with one level of nesting
- Connection surfaces with geometry, seam definition and 2–3 types (bonded, clearance, bolted flange)
- Node graph with field and mesh types; lattice, TPMS, shell, scatter and instancing nodes
- All seven design rules enforced, with the diagnostics listed above
- Dependency-graph scheduler, caching, background regeneration with previews
- 3MF, STL and STEP export (procedural volumes as mesh bodies); STEP and STL import

**Later**

- Composite volumes, deep nesting, procedures that emit child volumes
- Configurations and design tables driving procedure parameters
- Drawings with disposable dimensions on generated contents
- Assemblies and mates to connection surfaces
- Approximate mesh-to-B-rep conversion
- Automatically easing a layout to satisfy connection surfaces (a convenience layered on top)
- Cloud generation for heavy procedures
- User-defined nodes (scripting), once the app is basically stable

**Out of scope**

- Volumes negotiating or changing their connection surfaces
- Overlapping volumes, or contents that ignore intrusions
- References into a volume's contents from outside it, for any kind of volume
- Simulation (FEA, CFD) beyond the published outputs, until a later product decision

## Open questions and risks

The kernel is decided: Open CASCADE (OCCT), adapted as needed. Most of the remaining risks are in seam conformity and in how fast generation runs.

**Kernel decision: Open CASCADE.** Parasolid's free evaluation is offered only to developers of commercial software, with no personal-use option ([Siemens](https://resources.sw.siemens.com/en-US/parasolid-free-evaluation)), and ACIS is likewise a commercial licence. OCCT is free under LGPL, and since the tool is never distributed, its licence places no obligations on this project.

| OCCT area | How the studio uses it | Adaptation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sketches, features, B-rep | Layout, boundaries, parametric volumes | Wrap behind an internal kernel interface; never expose OCCT types to volumes |
| Booleans, fillets | Authored features only | Known weak spots: validate results, fall back with clear errors |
| Tolerances | Authored geometry | OCCT tolerances are per entity and can grow; check them against the studio's global tolerance after each feature |
| Tessellation | B-rep volumes' display and output | Must follow fixed seam edges; likely needs a custom pass |
| STEP / IGES | Import and export | Use as-is |

The kernel interface stays small because procedural contents never touch B-rep. For mesh booleans inside procedures, a dedicated mesh library such as Manifold (Apache 2.0) is a better fit than OCCT.

**Risks**

- **Seam conformity:** every mesher must follow the seam definition exactly, or welding fails. This needs a shared seam library and heavy tests.
- **Generation speed:** lattices across large volumes can take seconds. Previews and caching must make that feel acceptable.
- **User habits:** muscle memory from other CAD tools (editing anything directly) will pull against encapsulation. The UI should make the right action obvious, even for its author.
- **Determinism on the GPU:** floating-point reductions on the GPU may not be bit-reproducible. Hashing outputs may need quantisation or ordered reductions.
